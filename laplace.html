CHAPTER 1

Laplace Transform

The big idea: turn a differential equation in time \(t\) into an algebra problem in \(s\). Solve the algebra, then transform back. Every topic below has a picture you can play with.

1.0 What the Laplace transform actually does

Definition 1.1 — the "weighted area" machine

Definition \[F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}=\int_0^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt\] for every \(s\) for which the improper integral converges.

Read it as a recipe. Take your signal \(f(t)\). Multiply it by a decaying exponential \(e^{-st}\). Measure the total area under the product. That single number is \(F(s)\). Change \(s\) and you get a different number, so \(F\) is a new function of \(s\).

Laplace machine: watch the area become F(s)
f(t)e−stf(t)e−st (area = F(s))
See itDrag \(s\) to the left until \(s\) is close to \(a\) with \(f=e^{at}\). The yellow area explodes. That is why \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}=\frac{1}{s-a}\) needs \(\operatorname{Re}(s)>a\). The integral only converges when the exponential beats the growth of \(f\).

General formula (1.4) \[\mathcal{L}\{y^{(n)}\}=s^nY(s)-s^{n-1}y(0)-s^{n-2}y'(0)-\cdots-y^{(n-1)}(0)\] The powers of \(s\) count down as the derivatives at 0 count up.

Exam proof: L{f''} = s²F − s f(0) − f'(0) (2025 Q1(a)(ii), 6 marks)

Start with the first derivative. Integrate by parts with \(u=e^{-st}\), \(dv=f'(t)dt\): \[\mathcal{L}\{f'\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f'(t)\,dt=\Big[e^{-st}f(t)\Big]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt\]
Because \(f\) is of exponential order \(a\), \(|f(t)|\le Me^{at}\). So for \(\operatorname{Re}s>a\), \(e^{-st}f(t)\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\). The bracket gives \(0-f(0)\): \[\mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0)\]
Apply the same result to \(g=f'\): \[\mathcal{L}\{f''\}=s\,\mathcal{L}\{f'\}-f'(0)=s\big(sF(s)-f(0)\big)-f'(0)\]
\[\boxed{\mathcal{L}\{f''(t)\}=s^2F(s)-sf(0)-f'(0)},\qquad \operatorname{Re}(s)>a\]

Exam proof: L{∫₀ᵗ f(u)du} = F(s)/s (2021 Q1(a)(ii), 5 marks)

Let \(g(t)=\int_0^t f(u)\,du\). Then \(g(0)=0\) and by the fundamental theorem of calculus \(g'(t)=f(t)\).
Apply the derivative rule to \(g\): \(\mathcal{L}\{g'\}=s\,\mathcal{L}\{g\}-g(0)=s\,\mathcal{L}\{g\}\).
But \(\mathcal{L}\{g'\}=\mathcal{L}\{f\}=F(s)\). So \(F(s)=s\,\mathcal{L}\{g\}\), which gives \(\boxed{\mathcal{L}\left\{\int_0^t f(u)du\right\}=\dfrac{F(s)}{s}}\).

1.4 Multiplying and dividing by t

Multiplying by \(t\) means differentiate in \(s\). Dividing by \(t\) means integrate in \(s\).

Theorem 1.13\[\mathcal{L}\{t^nf(t)\}=(-1)^n\frac{d^n}{ds^n}F(s)\]
Theorem 1.16\[\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{f(t)}t\right\}=\int_s^\infty F(p)\,dp\]

Exam proof: division by t (2021 Q1(a)(i), 5 marks)

Write the right-hand side using the definition of \(F\): \[\int_s^\infty F(p)\,dp=\int_s^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-pt}\,dt\,dp\]
Swap the order of integration (allowed for convergent integrals): \[=\int_0^\infty f(t)\left(\int_s^\infty e^{-pt}dp\right)dt\]
The inner integral is \(\left[\frac{-e^{-pt}}{t}\right]_{p=s}^{\infty}=\frac{e^{-st}}{t}\).
\[\int_0^\infty \frac{f(t)}{t}e^{-st}\,dt=\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{f(t)}{t}\right\}\quad\blacksquare\]

Using both rules (2021 Q1(b), 10 marks)

Spot the pattern: \(\int_0^\infty e^{-t}g(t)\,dt\) is just \(\mathcal{L}\{g\}\) at \(s=1\). Here \(g(t)=\int_0^t\frac{\sin u}{u}du\).
Division rule: \(\mathcal{L}\{\frac{\sin t}t\}=\int_s^\infty\frac{dp}{p^2+1}=\frac\pi2-\tan^{-1}s=\tan^{-1}\frac1s\).
Integral rule: \(\mathcal{L}\{g\}=\frac1s\tan^{-1}\frac1s\). Put \(s=1\): \(\ \tan^{-1}1=\boxed{\pi/4}\).
For \(\mathcal{L}\left\{\int_0^{t/2}\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{x}dx\right\}\): first \(\mathcal{L}\{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{t}\}=\int_s^\infty\left(\frac1p-\frac1{p+2}\right)dp=\ln\frac{s+2}{s}\).
Integral rule: \(G(s)=\frac1s\ln\left(1+\frac2s\right)\) for \(g(t)=\int_0^t\). Then the time-scaling rule with \(\alpha=\frac12\): \(\mathcal{L}\{g(t/2)\}=2G(2s)=\frac{2}{2s}\ln\left(1+\frac1s\right)=\boxed{\frac{\ln(1+1/s)}{s}}\).
Note on the paperThe identity \(\ln(1+1/s)/s\) only works if the integrand is \(\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{x}\). The scanned paper looks like \(e^{2x}\); with \(e^{+2x}\) you would get \(\ln(1-1/s)/s\). State your reading in the script if this appears.
Examples 1.15 and 1.18 \(\mathcal{L}\{t\sinh at\}=-\frac{d}{ds}\frac{a}{s^2-a^2}=\frac{2as}{(s^2-a^2)^2}\) · \(\mathcal{L}\{t^2\cos at\}=\frac{d^2}{ds^2}\frac{s}{s^2+a^2}=\frac{2s(s^2-3a^2)}{(s^2+a^2)^3}\)

1.6 Dirac delta: an infinitely tall, infinitely thin spike of area 1

The 2022 paper builds δ as the limit of rectangles δₙ. Watch it happen.

Definition 1.25 \[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)\,dx=1,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\delta(x-a)\,dx=f(a)\] It is a distribution (a generalized function), not an ordinary function.
δₙ(t) = n on [a − 1/2n, a + 1/2n]: area stays 1, the integral of f·δₙ tends to f(a)
δₙ(t)f(t)f·δₙ area

Exam proof (2022 Q1(a)–(b), 12 marks)

\(\delta_n\) is zero outside \([a-\frac1{2n},a+\frac1{2n}]\). So \[\int_0^\infty f(t)\delta_n(t)\,dt=n\int_{a-1/2n}^{a+1/2n}f(t)\,dt\quad(n\text{ large so the interval is inside }(0,\infty)).\]
Mean value theorem for integrals (\(f\) continuous): there is \(c_n\) in the interval with \(\int_{a-1/2n}^{a+1/2n}f\,dt=f(c_n)\cdot\frac1n\). So the integral equals \(f(c_n)\).
As \(n\to\infty\), the interval shrinks to \(a\), so \(c_n\to a\) and by continuity \(f(c_n)\to f(a)\): \[\int_0^\infty f(t)\delta(t-a)\,dt=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(c_n)=f(a)\]
Take \(f(t)=e^{-st}\), which is continuous at \(a\): \[\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\delta(t-a)\,dt=\boxed{e^{-as}}\] With \(a=0\) this gives \(\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t)\}=1\). Example 1.30: \(\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-5)e^{-3t}\}=e^{-15}e^{-5s}\).
