CHAPTER 4

Series Solutions, Frobenius, Bessel and Legendre

When an ODE has variable coefficients like \(xy''+y'+xy=0\), no elementary formula solves it. So we guess a power series, plug it in, and let the equation tell us every coefficient. This topic appears in every past paper, worth 25 marks.

4.1 Ordinary, regular singular and irregular singular points

Definition 4.1 for \(P_0(x)y''+P_1(x)y'+P_2(x)y=0\). Asked in 2021 Q2(a) and 2022 Q2(a).

Ordinary point\(P_0(a)\ne0\). Plain power series \(\sum a_n(x-a)^n\) works.
Regular singular\(P_0(a)=0\) but both \[p=(x-a)\frac{P_1}{P_0},\quad q=(x-a)^2\frac{P_2}{P_0}\] stay finite (analytic) at \(a\). Use Frobenius.
Irregular singular\(P_0(a)=0\) and \(p\) or \(q\) blows up at \(a\). Beyond the course.
"Analytic" in plain wordsIt can be written as a convergent power series \(\sum c_n(x-a)^n\) near \(a\). For a ratio of polynomials, that just means the factor \((x-a)\) cancels completely and nothing is left in the denominator.
Singular point detective: does (x − a)P₁/P₀ or (x − a)²P₂/P₀ explode?
p(x) = (x−a)P₁/P₀q(x) = (x−a)²P₂/P₀

The 4-step recipe, with the step everyone gets wrong (aligning powers) made visual

① Assume \(y=\sum a_nx^n\)
→
② Differentiate and substitute
→
③ Collect each power \(x^k\) and set it to 0
→
④ Recurrence gives \(a_2,a_3,\dots\) in terms of \(a_0,a_1\)
The index-shift trick (the red circles in your notes) \(y'=\sum_{n\ge1}na_nx^{n-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)a_{n+1}x^n\) and \(y''=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}x^n\).

4.3 Frobenius method about a regular singular point

Theorem 4.6: \(y=x^m\sum a_nx^n\) with \(a_0\ne0\). The new unknown \(m\) comes from the indicial equation.

Assume \(y=\sum a_nx^{n+m}\), so \(y'=\sum(n+m)a_nx^{n+m-1}\) and \(y''=\sum(n+m)(n+m-1)a_nx^{n+m-2}\).
Substitute and look at the lowest power of x. Its coefficient is \(a_0\cdot I(m)\). Since \(a_0\ne0\), \(I(m)=0\). This quadratic is the indicial equation.
The rest of the powers give the recurrence, so \(a_1,a_2,\dots\) follow in terms of \(a_0\).
The two roots \(m_1\ge m_2\) decide the form of the second solution (cards below).
Case 1 · \(m_1-m_2\notin\mathbb Z\)Two plain Frobenius series:
\(y_1=x^{m_1}\sum a_nx^n\), \(y_2=x^{m_2}\sum b_nx^n\)
Case 2 · \(m_1=m_2\)A log is guaranteed:
\(y_2=y_1\ln|x|+x^{m_1}\sum b_nx^n\)
Case 3 · \(m_1-m_2=N\in\mathbb Z^+\)\(y_2=C\,y_1\ln|x|+x^{m_2}\sum b_nx^n\).
Sometimes \(C=0\), giving no log.

Remark 4.9If \(m\) is not an integer, \(x^m\) is only real for \(x>0\). Work on \(x>0\), and treat \(x<0\) separately if needed. The same applies to \(x>a\) for a point \(x=a\).

4.4 Bessel's equation and Bessel functions

\(x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0\): drums, circular pipes, anything round

Bessel function of the first kind \[J_n(x)=\sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^r}{r!\,(n+r)!}\left(\frac x2\right)^{2r+n}\]
\(x=0\) is regular singular: \(p=1\) and \(q=x^2-\nu^2\) are both polynomials.
Indicial: \(m(m-1)+m-\nu^2=0\Rightarrow m=\pm\nu\).
Recurrence: \(a_k\big((m+k)^2-\nu^2\big)=-a_{k-2}\). With \(m=\nu\): \(a_k=-\dfrac{a_{k-2}}{k(k+2\nu)}\), and the odd coefficients are 0.
So \(a_{2r}=\dfrac{(-1)^ra_0}{2^{2r}r!(\nu+1)(\nu+2)\cdots(\nu+r)}\). Choosing \(a_0=\frac1{2^\nu\nu!}\) gives exactly \(J_\nu\).
\(\nu=0\) gives \(xy''+y'+xy=0\) (divide by \(x\)). Equal roots, so Case 2: \[y_1=J_0=1-\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{x^4}{64}-\cdots,\quad y_2=J_0\ln x+\frac{x^2}{4}-\frac{3x^4}{128}+\frac{11x^6}{13824}-\cdots\]

4.5 Legendre polynomials

\((1-x^2)y''-2xy'+n(n+1)y=0\): one of the two series stops and becomes a polynomial

Rodrigues' formula\[P_n(x)=\frac1{2^nn!}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^2-1)^n\]normalised so that \(P_n(1)=1\).
Orthogonality on [−1, 1]\[\int_{-1}^1P_mP_n\,dx=0\ (m\ne n),\qquad\int_{-1}^1P_n^2\,dx=\frac{2}{2n+1}\]
Legendre polynomials and the Rodrigues machine

\((x^2-1)^5=x^{10}-5x^8+10x^6-10x^4+5x^2-1\)
Differentiate 5 times: \(\frac{10!}{5!}x^5-5\frac{8!}{3!}x^3+10\frac{6!}{1!}x=30240x^5-33600x^3+7200x\)
Divide by \(2^5\cdot5!=3840\): \(\boxed{P_5(x)=\tfrac18\left(63x^5-70x^3+15x\right)}\)
